The Ocean and Climate Change

A
The world's oceans cover approximately 71 percent of Earth's surface and play a central role in regulating the planet's climate. They have absorbed more than 90 percent of the excess heat generated by human greenhouse gas emissions since the pre-industrial era and approximately one-third of the carbon dioxide released by burning fossil fuels. Without the ocean's vast heat and carbon absorption capacity, Earth's surface temperature would already have risen far above currently observed levels. Yet this moderating function comes at a profound cost: the ocean itself is being fundamentally altered by the heat and carbon it has absorbed.

B
Ocean warming is now measurable throughout the water column, though it is most pronounced in the upper 700 metres. Warmer surface water is less dense than colder deep water, which means it sits on top and resists mixing – a process called stratification. Stratification reduces the vertical transport of nutrients from the cold, nutrient-rich deep ocean to the sunlit surface layers where phytoplankton grow. Since phytoplankton are the foundation of most marine food webs and responsible for roughly half of all photosynthesis on Earth, reduced nutrient supply has cascading ecological consequences extending from zooplankton through fish to marine mammals and seabirds.

C
Ocean acidification – caused by the absorption of atmospheric carbon dioxide, which dissolves in seawater to form carbonic acid – is an equally serious concern. Since the industrial revolution, the average pH of the ocean's surface layer has fallen from approximately 8.2 to 8.1. Though this seems a small change, the pH scale is logarithmic, meaning that a 0.1 unit fall represents a roughly 26 percent increase in hydrogen ion concentration. This acidification threatens the ability of shell-forming marine organisms – corals, molluscs, sea urchins, pteropods – to build and maintain their calcium carbonate structures, since more acidic water dissolves carbonate ions more rapidly.

D
Coral reefs, which cover less than one percent of the ocean floor but support approximately 25 percent of all marine species, are particularly vulnerable. Bleaching events, in which corals expel their symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) under thermal stress and turn white, have increased dramatically in frequency and severity since the 1980s. The Great Barrier Reef experienced mass bleaching events in 2016, 2017, 2020 and 2022 – an unprecedented succession that has left large portions permanently damaged. Severely bleached corals can recover if temperatures return to normal within weeks, but repeated or prolonged bleaching leads to mortality. Scientists project that at 1.5°C of global warming, approximately 70 to 90 percent of coral reefs would be seriously degraded; at 2°C, the figure rises to over 99 percent.

E
Sea level rise represents a third major consequence of ocean warming. Thermal expansion of water – the fact that water occupies more volume as it heats up – accounts for roughly one third of observed sea level rise. Melting of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets and mountain glaciers accounts for the remainder. Global mean sea level has risen by approximately 20 cm since 1900, and the rate of rise is accelerating. Projections for 2100 range widely, from around 0.3 metres under aggressive emission reduction scenarios to more than one metre under high-emission pathways. Low-lying island nations and densely populated coastal megacities face existential and practical threats from rising seas and associated increases in storm surge intensity.

F
The impact of ocean warming on fisheries is a matter of growing concern for food security. Many commercially important fish species are shifting their ranges poleward as surface waters warm, moving into new areas and vacating others. The displacement of species such as Atlantic cod from traditional fishing grounds has economic, cultural and nutritional consequences for coastal communities that have depended on them for generations. In tropical regions, declining fish populations linked to coral reef degradation threaten the food security of hundreds of millions of people. Marine aquaculture – fish and shellfish farming – is also being affected, as warming, acidification and disease associated with altered ocean conditions create new challenges for producers.

G
Addressing ocean change requires both mitigation – reducing greenhouse gas emissions to slow warming and acidification – and adaptation – helping communities and ecosystems adjust to changes that are already underway. Marine protected areas, which restrict harmful activities in designated ocean zones, can help ecosystems maintain resilience and recover from disturbance. Restoration programmes for degraded habitats, including coral and seagrass, are underway in several regions, though their scalability relative to the extent of damage remains a significant challenge. Most scientists and policymakers agree that without substantial emissions reductions, adaptation measures alone cannot prevent the most damaging ocean changes from occurring.
