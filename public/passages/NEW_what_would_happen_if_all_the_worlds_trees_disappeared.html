What Would Happen If All the World's Trees Disappeared?
How dependent are we on the survival of forests?

A 'Forests are the lifeline of our world,' says Meg Lowman, director of the Tree Foundation, a non-profit organisation that is dedicated to tree research and education. Yet we often treat trees as disposable, something to be harvested for economic gain, or as an obstacle to our development. Since we began practising agriculture around 12,000 years ago, we've cleared nearly half of the world's trees, according to a study published in the journal Nature. In many places, this loss is accelerating.

B Barring an unimaginable catastrophe, however, there's no scenario under which we would fell every tree on the planet. But imagining such a dystopian world can help us appreciate just how lost we would be without them. 'They are irreplaceable,' says Isabel Rosa, a lecturer in environmental data. 'If we get rid of all the trees, we will live [on] a planet that might not actually be able to sustain us anymore.' If trees disappeared overnight, so would much of the planet's life. Habitat loss is already the primary driver of species loss worldwide, so the destruction of all remaining forests would be catastrophic for plants, animals, fungi and more, says Jayme Prevedello, an ecologist at Rio de Janeiro State University in Brazil.

C The wave of extinctions would extend beyond forests, depleting in particular those species that depend on single or small groups of trees. Prevedello and his colleagues have found, for example, that overall biodiversity is 50-100% higher in places with even scattered trees than in open areas. 'Even a single, isolated tree in an open treeless area can act as a species "magnet," attracting and providing resources for many animals and plants,' Prevedello says. 'Therefore, losing even individual trees can severely impact biodiversity locally.'

D The planet's climate would also be drastically altered in the short and long term. A key function of trees is to facilitate the water cycle. They pump water from the soil and, by a process called evapotranspiration, they use solar energy to convert this liquid water into vapour and deposit it into the atmosphere, thereby contributing to cloud formation and precipitation. Trees buffer coastal populations from storm surges and prevent flooding by trapping water rather than letting it rush into rivers. In addition, they keep soil in place that would otherwise be swept into the oceans, smothering the sea floor and suffocating coral reefs. Islands stripped of trees would lose their barriers to the ocean, and many, some heavily populated, would simply vanish. 'Removing trees means losing huge amounts of land to the ocean,' says Thomas Crowther, a global systems ecologist and lead author of the Nature study.

E In addition to mediating the water cycle, trees have a localised cooling effect. They provide shade that maintains soil temperatures and, as the darkest thing in the landscape, they absorb heat rather than reflect it. Prevedello believes that, without these cooling services, most places where trees formerly stood would immediately see significant temperature rises. He and his colleagues found that complete removal of a 25 sq km patch of forest caused local annual temperatures to increase by at least 2°C in equatorial regions and 1°C in temperate areas.

F Globally, trees act as an important 'carbon sink', in that they combat climatic warming by storing carbon in their trunks and removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. While deforestation alone accounts for 13% of total global carbon emissions at their present levels, the new uses that the same cleared land is put to — often agriculture and industry — then account for 23% of emissions. With all trees on the planet wiped out, previously forested ecosystems 'would become only a source of emission of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere, rather than a sink,' says professor of environmental science Paolo D'Odorico.

G Over time, Crowther predicts, we could see the release of 450 gigatonnes of carbon into the atmosphere — more than doubling the amount already contributed by humans. Large amounts of carbon would also run into the oceans, causing extreme acidification and possibly killing everything but jellyfish, he says. For a while, this effect would be held in check by smaller plants and grasses. But while smaller plants capture carbon at a faster rate than trees, they also release it more rapidly. Eventually — perhaps over a few decades — the system would break down. 'The timeline depends on where you are, since decomposition is much faster in the tropics than the Arctic,' D'Odorico says. 'But once carbon dioxide is in the atmosphere, it doesn't matter if it's coming from here or from there.'

H The loss of trees would also be mourned on a deeper level. Trees are an important part of countless childhoods and feature heavily in art, literature, poetry, music and more. They have had spiritual significance since prehistory and still play prominent roles in major religions today. Although we put a price on many aspects of life, one thing we have never done is to come up with a monetary figure for the spiritual and societal importance of forests.

I All told, we would struggle to live in a world without trees. Urbanised living would quickly become a thing of the past, as large population centres became unsustainable. Any remaining populations, Meg Lowman believes, would probably be those that have retained an understanding about how to live in harsh, barren environments, such as Australia's Aboriginals. Crowther, on the other hand, suspects that life would only persist in high-tech colonies. 'Even if we could live in a world without trees, who would want to?' he says.
