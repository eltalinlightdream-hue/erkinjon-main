Georgia O'Keeffe: America's Desert Painter

A
Georgia O'Keeffe (1887–1986) is widely regarded as the most important American woman artist of the twentieth century. Her richly coloured, large-scale paintings of flowers, bones and the New Mexico landscape established her as a defining figure in American modernism, yet she always resisted the labels that critics and curators attempted to attach to her. She was not, she insisted, a member of the Stieglitz circle, not a feminist, not an abstractionist – she was simply an artist who painted what she saw and felt. This fierce independence of classification is itself central to her identity and her legacy.

B
O'Keeffe was born in rural Wisconsin and showed artistic promise from childhood. She studied at the Art Institute of Chicago and later at the Art Students League in New York, where she received rigorous training in the academic tradition. It was not until she encountered the ideas of Arthur Wesley Dow, who emphasised the expressive arrangement of form and colour over literal representation, that her work began to take its distinctive direction. Dow's influence shifted O'Keeffe's focus from technical mastery to emotional and artistic expression, and it was this shift that would come to define her mature style.

C
O'Keeffe sent a series of abstract charcoal drawings to a friend in New York, who showed them to Alfred Stieglitz, the influential photographer and gallery owner who ran the 291 Gallery. Stieglitz was so impressed that he exhibited them without O'Keeffe's prior knowledge. When she confronted him about this, their encounter sparked a relationship – intellectual, professional and eventually romantic – that would shape both their careers. Stieglitz promoted O'Keeffe's work energetically throughout the 1920s, and his photographs of her became some of the most celebrated portrait photographs in American art history.

D
O'Keeffe's large-scale flower paintings, begun in the 1920s, are among her most recognised works. By painting single flowers at enormous scale – filling the canvas with a single bloom – she compelled viewers to look closely at forms they habitually overlooked. She consistently denied the sexual interpretations that critics, influenced by Freudian theory, applied to these works, arguing that she was simply making people see what they had never taken the trouble to look at before. The flowers, she said, were about attention: the quality of looking, not the content of symbolism.

E
In 1929, O'Keeffe made her first visit to New Mexico, and the desert landscapes of the American Southwest transformed her work. She was immediately captivated by the region's vastness, its extraordinary light and the bleached skull bones she found scattered on the desert floor. Where other artists might have seen these bones as symbols of death and desolation, O'Keeffe saw them as embodying the desert's essential character: stark, elemental and enduring. Animal skulls and pelvic bones – often floating against vivid blue skies or flower-filled backgrounds – became signature motifs in her paintings from this period.

F
After Stieglitz's death in 1946, O'Keeffe moved permanently to New Mexico, settling first at the Ghost Ranch property she had been renting for years and later at a second property in the village of Abiquiú. She lived and worked in the desert for decades, developing an increasingly austere and meditative style as she aged. Visitors, including artists, writers and cultural figures from across the world, made pilgrimages to her remote home. Her solitude was not hermitism: she maintained a wide correspondence, travelled internationally, and remained sharply engaged with contemporary art and culture into extreme old age.
