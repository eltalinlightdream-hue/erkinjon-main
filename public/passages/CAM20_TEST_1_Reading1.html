The Kākāpō – New Zealand's Night Parrot

The kākāpō (Strigops habroptilus) is a large, nocturnal, flightless parrot endemic to New Zealand. At up to 4 kg, it is the heaviest parrot in the world. Its name derives from the Māori language and means 'night parrot'. Unlike most birds, it has a low metabolic rate and a relatively long lifespan, often living for decades in the wild. It is also the only parrot known to have a lek mating system, in which males gather to compete for female attention through elaborate displays and booming calls.

The kākāpō was once abundant throughout New Zealand, but the arrival of humans – first Polynesian settlers and later European colonists – caused catastrophic population declines. Polynesians hunted kākāpō for food and for their soft, dense feathers, which were woven into cloaks. Europeans then introduced a suite of predators, including rats, stoats and cats, which preyed on kākāpō eggs, chicks and even adults. By the late nineteenth century, kākāpō had disappeared from much of their former range on the North and South Islands.

The kākāpō's diet is exclusively plant-based. It eats a wide variety of native vegetation, including leaves, stems, flowers, nectar and seeds. It is particularly fond of rimu (Dacrydium cupressinum), a native conifer, and uses its strong beak to extract the oily, nutrient-rich fruits. The bird also uses its strong claws and beak to dig up plant bulbs and tubers, which provide energy during leaner periods. Unlike many birds, kākāpō do not cache food – they consume it directly.

Kākāpō breeding is unusual in several respects. It is tied to mast years, when certain trees – especially rimu – produce an unusually abundant crop of fruit. In these years, males construct shallow bowl-shaped depressions in the soil on ridgelines and hilltops, then begin booming – a series of deep, resonant calls that can carry several kilometres through the forest at night. Females travel to the booming sites, select a mate, and then raise their chicks entirely alone. The dependency of breeding on mast years means that kākāpō may not breed at all for several consecutive years.

Eggs are white and typically hatch after about 30 days of incubation. Chicks are covered with a layer of soft, pale feathers (down), which keeps them warm during the cold nights while the mother forages. However, because the mother must leave to feed herself each night, chicks are vulnerable to predators during these absences. Predation of chicks by stoats and rats during nesting is one of the most significant causes of reproductive failure in the species.

By 1977, only 18 individuals had been found on Fiordland, and these were all male. An additional population was then discovered in 1977 on Stewart Island (Rakiura), but stoats and cats posed severe threats there too. Beginning in approximately 1980, conservation managers began moving all surviving kākāpō to predator-free islands – first Codfish Island (Whenua Hou) and later Anchor Island. This translocation programme marked the formal beginning of intensively managed recovery.

The Kākāpō Recovery Programme, which brings together the New Zealand Department of Conservation, Māori iwi and a range of other partners, has driven remarkable progress. A central challenge has been securing consistent funding to maintain around-the-clock monitoring, supplementary feeding and veterinary care across multiple island sanctuaries. Nest monitoring technology, including miniature cameras installed above nest burrows, has enabled managers to intervene when chicks are in danger. Equally important has been the engagement of diverse stakeholders – including local communities, donors, researchers and government agencies – whose collective commitment has made the programme one of the world's most successful conservation efforts.

As of 2024, the kākāpō population stands at over 240 individuals, the highest number recorded since modern conservation management began. Genetic management has also become central: because all living kākāpō descend from a tiny founding population, managers carefully plan pairings to maintain maximum genetic diversity. Despite the progress, the species remains critically endangered, and continued vigilance against disease, inbreeding and ecological disruption on island sanctuaries is essential.
