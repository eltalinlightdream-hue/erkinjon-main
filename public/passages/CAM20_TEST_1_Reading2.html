The Remarkable Elm

A
The elm (Ulmus) has long held a special place in the landscapes and cultural heritage of Europe and North America. For centuries, these tall, graceful trees defined village greens, cathedral precincts and country estates. Their domed canopies, reaching heights of 40 metres or more, offered shade in summer and structural grandeur in winter. Artists and writers celebrated the elm as an emblem of rural England; Thomas Constable's paintings are filled with them. Yet within a few decades of the mid-twentieth century, the elm – particularly Ulmus procera, the English elm – was brought to the brink of functional extinction across much of its range by a devastating fungal disease.

B
Dutch elm disease, caused by the fungus Ophiostoma novo-ulmi, was not, despite its name, a Dutch invention. It was named after the Dutch botanists who first identified and studied it in the 1920s. The pathogen blocks the elm's water-conducting vessels, causing the tree to wilt and die within a growing season. It is spread primarily by elm bark beetles of the genus Scolytus, which carry fungal spores from infected to healthy trees as they feed beneath the bark. The disease arrived in Britain in the 1960s, carried on infected timber imported from North America, and spread at a rate of roughly 40 kilometres per year. By the mid-1980s an estimated 25 million elm trees had died in Britain alone.

C
Efforts to contain the outbreak met with limited success. Sanitation felling – the rapid removal and burning of infected trees before beetles could emerge and spread – worked well when implemented quickly and systematically, as in the city of Brighton and the town of Tenterden, both of which retained significant elm populations thanks to vigilant management. However, elms spread naturally through root suckers rather than seeds, meaning large woodland stands are often genetically identical clones. Once the disease entered a clonal group, it could pass underground through connected root systems from tree to tree without any beetle involvement.

D
The ecological consequences were severe. Elm woodland provided habitat for numerous specialist invertebrates, including the white-letter hairstreak butterfly (Satyrium w-album), whose caterpillars feed exclusively on elm. As mature elms died, this butterfly's populations fell sharply. Elm seeds and fruits (known as samaras) are an important food source for birds such as finches during winter, and the loss of mature trees removed a significant resource from hedgerow and woodland ecosystems. The decomposing trunks of dead elms have, however, provided microhabitats for saproxylic (dead-wood-dependent) beetles, partially offsetting ecological losses.

E
Elm wood itself has remarkable properties. It is highly resistant to splitting and warping when submerged in water, properties that made it the preferred material for the keels of wooden sailing ships and for the hubs of wagon wheels. Medieval London was supplied with water through elm pipes: hollowed-out elm trunks were laid end-to-end beneath the city's streets. Elm was also prized for coffins, butcher's blocks and, in finely figured form (known as burr elm), for furniture making. Flooring manufacturers valued elm for its interlocked grain, which resists the denting and wear to which softer woods are prone.

F
As elm has become scarce, craftspeople and manufacturers have increasingly turned to alternatives. Oak (Quercus) has filled some of the gap in structural and furniture applications, though it is denser and more expensive. Ash (Fraxinus excelsior), itself now under threat from ash dieback disease caused by the fungus Hymenoscyphus fraxineus, has been used in tool handles and sports equipment where elm's toughness was previously exploited. The scarcity of elm has given any surviving pieces of elm furniture and woodwork considerable commercial and heritage value.

G
Researchers have been working for decades to develop disease-resistant elm cultivars. Several European elm species, notably Ulmus minor and Ulmus glabra, show partial natural resistance, and breeders have created hybrids combining resistance with good ornamental form. The 'Lutece' and 'Columella' cultivars, developed by the French National Institute for Agriculture, Food and the Environment (INRAE), have been planted in urban settings across France and elsewhere with considerable success. In Britain, the Conservation Foundation's Great British Elm Experiment is trialling resistant cultivars at hundreds of sites, with the aim of restoring elms to the countryside. Whether any cultivar will prove truly resistant under the disease pressures of a warming climate remains to be seen.
