Listening Script – Section 4

Part 4. You will hear a lecture about invasive species on islands. Now listen carefully and answer questions thirty-one to forty.

Good morning. Today I want to discuss one of the most serious conservation challenges facing island ecosystems – invasive species.

Islands present a particular problem. Species on islands evolved in isolation over thousands of years without exposure to certain predators or competitors. When new species are introduced, usually through human activity, the consequences can be catastrophic.

The most destructive animals that humans have inadvertently introduced to islands are rats and snakes. Rats arrived on ships for centuries, and snakes – like the brown tree snake in Guam – have been introduced accidentally in cargo.

The effects are wide-ranging. First, there's economic damage – the decline in the tourism industry. When an island's unique wildlife disappears, visitors stop coming. Second, there are direct safety issues. In some island territories, the increase in feral animal populations has led to more traffic collisions between vehicles and animals crossing roads. Third, some invasive species – particularly feral pigs and goats – destroy root systems, leading to soil erosion and ultimately a loss of rain because forests that generate rainfall are destroyed.

Traditional control methods involve applying poison – either aerially or in bait stations. This works, but can have serious unintended consequences. The main risk is that removing one species accidentally removes what ecologists call a building block – a species that was actually holding another invasive species in check.

Let me give you a case study. On Lord Howe Island, when rats were successfully removed, there was a recovery in the population of the Lord Howe petrel – a dog bird, as locals call it. A genuine conservation success.

On Macquarie Island, researchers are experimenting with using noise – recorded predator calls – to disrupt invasive species and drive them toward capture areas.

Increasingly, conservationists agree that the best results come from a combination of different techniques rather than relying on any single method.

Thank you. That is the end of Part 4.
